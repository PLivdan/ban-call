The ban phase as a game

Ban Call treats the ban phase as a game where some of the cards are face down. The page knows the lobby: the rank, the map, who bans first and the heroes on show. It doesn't know who is playing. So the model draws the players it can't see, many times over, and plays the ban phase through for each draw. The figure follows one lobby through three of those draws, then folds them into the one tree the page can see.

Three draws the page can't tell apart

Each draw is a guess at who is really in the lobby, and gets a lane of its own. Bands run through the bans from left to right, as tall as their share of the games. The dashed lines join the points where the page sees exactly the same thing. The numbers are made up to show the arithmetic, and each one is worked out from the ones to its right. Hover anything in the figure for its numbers.

The recursion, term by term

The whole method fits in three lines. Hover a term to find it in the figure.

After six bans V6(s6) = 𝔼θ | s6[1/282 Σi,j P(WA = 1 | LAi, LBj, m)] Both teams draft 28 lineups from the players in the draw and every pairing is scored. Then the page averages over the draws, weighted by how likely each one is given what it has seen.
Your turn Vk(sk) = maxb ∈ 𝓛(sk)V̄k+1(skb) The best of every legal ban. Each one is scored by the average of ten networks (the bar), and the networks see only what the page sees (sk), so the choice is the same in every draw.
Their turn Vk(sk) = 𝔼θ | sk[Σb σ̂B(b | sk, θB) Vk+1(skb)] Not their worst ban for you, their likely one. Their ban model gives the odds of each ban for the players they might be, and the values are weighted by those odds.

Reading the tree

Game theory draws a game like this as a tree. Here it runs left to right: first chance (the draw) picks who is really playing, then come the bans, blue for yours and red for theirs. Each draw gets its own copy of the tree, because their bans depend on who they are.

The dashed lines are the heart of it. Where they join two points, the page sees exactly the same lobby and bans, so it has to make the same choice at both. It can't ban for draw 1 in draw 1 and for draw 2 in draw 2. It bans for the average draw. Their bans are different: their players know who they are, and teams keep their own heroes open. So each of their bans shifts the odds between the draws.

The page never works those odds out. Its networks learn from simulated games, and the games that reach a point already come from each draw in the right proportions. Averaging over the games is averaging over the draws.

What the figure leaves out

  • Three draws and a few bans per column stand in for thousands of draws and every legal ban. All of them are scored.
  • Each row's values come from networks that see only the lobby and the bans so far. They are fitted from the last ban back to the first, on simulated games.
  • Ten networks per row are fitted on different resamples of the lobbies. Your bans are scored by their average, and their spread is the range shown on the advice.
  • The same machinery runs three more times as checks: once with you banning like a typical team, once with them banning to hurt you most, and once with both teams banning like players who know their teams.
  • The page runs one small network per ban, trained to copy the ten.
  • Whether following the advice wins more real games is estimated on real matches set aside before fitting (a doubly robust off-policy estimate).
